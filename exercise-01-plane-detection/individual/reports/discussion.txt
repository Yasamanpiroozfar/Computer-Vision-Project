INDIVIDUAL EXERCISE 1 - DISCUSSION

1. MLESAC

For MLESAC, I replaced the binary RANSAC score with a residual-based cost. Points with a distance smaller than epsilon contribute their point-to-plane distance, while all other points contribute the constant gamma. The model with the lowest total cost is selected. I used gamma = 0.020 m, which is larger than all tested epsilon values.

I tested epsilon values of 0.004, 0.006, 0.008, 0.010 and 0.012 m on example4kinect. Each setting was repeated with five random seeds and 256 hypotheses. A floor plane estimated with a larger number of hypotheses was used as a common reference for the comparisons.

For the smaller epsilon values, RANSAC and MLESAC produced similar results. The difference was more visible at larger thresholds. At epsilon = 0.012 m, the mean offset difference was approximately 1.55 mm for RANSAC and 0.56 mm for MLESAC. The mean normal-angle difference was approximately 0.092 degrees for RANSAC and 0.080 degrees for MLESAC.

In this experiment, MLESAC was more stable when epsilon was increased. However, it was still affected by the selected threshold because a larger epsilon accepts more points as inliers.

Advantages:
- Inlier residuals are included in the score.
- Models with similar inlier counts can still be distinguished.
- The results were more stable for the larger epsilon values.

Disadvantages:
- Epsilon and gamma still need to be selected.
- The cost depends on the unit and scale of the residuals.
- Scoring requires more calculations than binary inlier counting.

2. PREEMPTIVE RANSAC

For Preemptive RANSAC, I first generated M plane hypotheses. The hypotheses were evaluated on blocks of B randomly ordered points, and their costs were accumulated after every block. The models were then ranked and the weaker hypotheses were removed using:

f(i) = floor(M * 2^(-floor(i/B)))

The process continued until only one hypothesis remained or all points had been evaluated. The hypotheses were scored using the MLESAC cost.

I tested M = 64, 256 and 1024 with B = 64, 256 and 1024, resulting in nine parameter combinations. Each combination was repeated with five random seeds.

For M = 64 and B = 64, the mean normal-angle difference from the reference was approximately 0.279 degrees and the mean offset difference was approximately 1.87 mm. With M = 1024 and B = 256, the values improved to approximately 0.079 degrees and 0.59 mm. Increasing M improved the chance of generating a good initial hypothesis.

The value of B determines how many points are used before the hypotheses are pruned. A small B reduces the number of evaluations, but the ranking is based on less evidence. A larger B gives a more reliable ranking, but increases the computational cost.

For M = 1024, increasing B from 256 to 1024 increased the mean number of model-point evaluations from approximately 0.52 million to 2.10 million. The mean angle difference changed only slightly from approximately 0.079 to 0.088 degrees. Based on these results, I selected M = 1024 and B = 256 as a suitable compromise between accuracy and computation time.

Advantages:
- Weak hypotheses are removed before being evaluated on the complete point cloud.
- M directly controls the initial number of hypotheses.
- It can require fewer model-point evaluations than evaluating every hypothesis on all points.

Disadvantages:
- A small M may not include a sufficiently good hypothesis.
- A small B can cause a good model to be removed too early.
- Removed hypotheses cannot return in later steps.
- Large M or B values reduce the computational advantage.
